_Welcome to "That's my (User) Story and I'm sticking to it!" Tech tales for the curious and short of attention span._

Picture this: you're an intern. You've spent weeks building something great. You walk into your boss's office to show it off. And on that very same day, one of the biggest companies on Earth releases the exact same thing.

How would you feel?

Crushed?
Embarrassed?
Ready to quit computers and take up goat farming?

Patrick Hammond has a different answer. But first, the story.

When I interviewed Patrick, he started with this: _"February 8th. A day I will never forget."_

Patrick is a consultant at Ingage Partners these days, working with me at a large insurance provider in Cincinnati. But in 2005, he was a student at the University of Missouri-Rolla _(now Missouri S&T)_, working as an intern at a government agency. He was using a GIS system, which is a fancy way of saying: maps and lots of data.

His assignment: take an existing Java Swing application and turn it into a web app.

The purpose of the app was to take archaeological data and make it navigable on a web interface. And not just _viewable_. Scrollable. Think dragging a map around with your mouse. That meant Patrick had to write all of the math to stitch images together so they'd line up seamlessly as you moved around.
He built it with Java Server Faces, a framework I'd honestly never heard of. _(If you haven't either, never mind. It's a Java thing. Stay with me.)_

And he finished! That's the part that impressed me. An intern, handed a desktop app, delivered a working web app with a scrollable map.

I asked him how he got so much done so quickly. His answer:

> "It was winter break, I was miles away from my family, and there were no girls around. I didn't have anything else to do!"

_(This is for sure one way that I can relate to him, having been an immature college man-child myself!)_

So, on February 8th, 2005, Patrick walked in to present his project to his boss.

Do you know what else happened on February 8th, 2005? Go ahead! Google it: `Web software released February 8 2005`

Google Maps launched.

Patrick's project was blown out of the water.

Now, I'll be honest: when I heard that, I winced for him. Weeks of work, upstaged by Google, on the _same day_. But Patrick didn't tell it like a tragedy. When I asked how he felt about it, he said:

> "The fact that Google made the same thing I was making was a huge confirmation that I made something valuable!"

I love that. Most of us would've seen "Google built it better" and stopped there. Patrick saw "Google built it" and concluded he'd been on the right track the whole time. _(That's either the healthiest attitude in software or a very well-practiced coping mechanism. Possibly both.)_

For me, the story isn't really about Google, though. It's about the intern.

Here was a student, miles from home, with a big problem and a lot of free time. Nobody hovering. Nobody saying "that's too ambitious for an intern." And he built something genuinely impressive, including the math, in a framework he was learning as he went.

Interns can be far more capable than their job title suggests. If you're lucky enough to have one, give them room to try stuff. You might get a web app out of it. And if you're really lucky, you might get a project so good that Google builds it too.

Patrick left the internship without tracking where the project went. He figured GIS would ship its own Google Maps-style tool soon enough, and that his app was probably redundant. But he took something more durable with him: proof that he could deliver high-caliber software. That confidence carried him all the way to founding his own company, Atomic Robot. _(Maybe we'll get more of that story later.)_

At Ingage, one of our values is to _amaze our client_. Patrick lived a version of that as an intern: handed a desktop app, he didn't just port it, he delivered a working web app with a scrollable map, in a framework he was still learning. That same instinct, to go past the brief and build something that earns a "wow," is what he brings to clients today.
